1 · The mapCan you draw the UK with pubs?

Yes, mostly. Every premises that serves food or drink is registered with the council for food hygiene inspections, and the Food Standards Agency publishes the whole register. I took every place registered as a pub, bar or nightclub, sorted out the social clubs, bowls clubs and Royal British Legions, and put a dot on each one.

The UK comes out surprisingly well. The cities glow, the valleys of South Wales show up as little strings of lights, and the Pennines, Dartmoor and the Cambrian Mountains appear as dark holes. The Scottish Highlands look almost empty. That's partly true and partly paperwork, which I come back to later.

2 · Before COVIDThe real last orders came in 2008

Everyone remembers the pubs shut by lockdown. The numbers say the big closing time came ten years earlier. ONS counts pub and bar workplaces that employ staff. In 2001 there were 52,500 in the UK. By 2019, before anyone had heard of furlough, there were 39,130. A quarter of the country's pubs went in the years when the smoking ban, the financial crash and supermarket beer all arrived at once.

Between 2008 and 2013 the UK lost more than 9,000 pubs, about five a day for five years. From 2019 to 2024, across lockdowns, energy bills and the price of a pint, the count fell by 1,260. Grants and furlough kept many doors registered, if not always open, but the COVID dip still looks like a hiccup next to 2009.

The bigger story is the size of the pubs. Small pubs, with fewer than ten staff, fell from 38,830 to 21,645. Big ones went up, from 13,675 to 16,225. We didn't lose the pub. We lost the little one on the corner and gained a large one with a laminated menu and a two-for-one on burgers.

3 · 2001 to 2019Where the taps ran dry

ONS also splits the count by council, from 2001 to 2019. Between those two years, 93% of UK councils lost pubs. London's outer boroughs lost the most: Barking and Dagenham went from 45 pubs to 15, which is two out of every three. Newham, Luton, Croydon and the East Lancashire towns of Burnley and Hyndburn all lost about half.

A handful of places went the other way, mostly where students, tourists or new bars arrived: Hackney, Newcastle, Blackpool, York and Brighton. The Highland figure also rose, which is a good clue that its almost-empty dot map is a paperwork problem.

4 · Pub namesOne Red Lion, two Red Lions…

There are 17,434 different pub names in the register, and the UK still can't stop calling its pubs the same five things. The Red Lion wins, as it has for centuries: about one pub in every hundred. It's followed by the Crown, the Royal Oak (named for the tree Charles II hid in after losing a battle, which is a very British thing to name a pub after) and the White Hart.

Animals beat royalty, which feels right. The Railway is sixth, a reminder that a lot of the country's pubs were built for people getting off trains.

5 · Pub crawlsStreets that are basically a pub crawl

Which streets have the most pubs and bars? I grouped every address by street and council, then threw out any street where the pubs are spread over more than 1.5 km, because that's not a crawl, that's a hike. Swansea's Wind Street fits 13 bars into 186 metres. That's one every 15 metres, so you can do the whole thing without finishing a pint.

    6 · The middle of nowhereHow far can you get from a pint?

    I laid a 1 km grid over the land, measured from every square to the nearest pub, then zoomed in on the worst spots at 100 m. In England the answer is 15.7 km, in Kielder Forest in Northumberland. Your nearest pint there is the Black Cock Inn in Falstone. In Wales it's 14 km, high in the Cambrian Mountains, where the nearest pub is the Trout Inn at Beulah.

    Why there's no Scottish answer. The Highland Council registers most of its pubs as hotels, because in much of the Highlands the pub is the hotel bar. Only 59 Highland premises are registered as pubs, against more than 750 hotels. Counting every hotel would add B&Bs with no bar at all, so for Scotland this measure would mostly tell you how the council files its paperwork. For Northern Ireland the furthest point lands right on the border, where the nearest pub is probably in Donegal and outside this data.

    About 5% of England is more than 5 km from a pub. In Wales it's 18%.

    Type a UK postcode. I use the middle of its postcode sector (for example, all of SW1A 1), count the pubs and bars within 800 metres, roughly a ten-minute walk, and compare that with every postcode in the UK.

    Data and method

    Pubs today: Food Standards Agency food hygiene rating data, downloaded October 2026, all 363 council files. I kept premises registered as "Pub/bar/nightclub", plus restaurants, hotels and other caterers whose names clearly say pub (Inn, Arms, Tavern, Bar…), which recovers pubs that councils file under another type. Names were then sorted into pubs, bars and clubs with simple word rules. About 7% of pubs have no location in the register and are left off the map. Some councils use the types differently, the Highlands most of all.

    2001 to 2024: ONS, Public houses and bars by local authority, 2001 to 2019, and Public houses and bars, London boroughs, 2001 to 2024 (which also gives UK totals). ONS counts workplaces with employees, so it is not the same thing as the FSA register, and I never subtract one from the other. Nationally they agree well (37,870 for ONS in 2024, 38,022 in the register today), but council by council they can differ a lot. Council figures are moved onto 2025 boundaries by adding up merged councils.
